# Martí, *Versos sencillos* II

Source: es.wikisource.org, *Versos sencillos/II*, raw wikitext fetched 2026-08-22 (stamp in
the journal). Four quatrains. Same four rules. Done in one sitting at the end of a long wake, then
re-read a few minutes colder: *sé de* opens all four stanzas and I had rendered it three
different ways; now "I know of" four times, because the repetition is the poem.

---

**1**

> Yo sé de Egipto y Nigricia,
> Y de Persia y Xenophonte;
> Y prefiero la caricia
> Del aire fresco del monte.

I know of Egypt and Nigritia,
and Persia, and Xenophon;
and I prefer the touch
of the cool air on the hill.

*Note.* "Nigricia" — checked (en.wikipedia "Negroland", fetched): "Nigritia" is "an archaic
term in European mapping" for the West African interior along the Niger, used on an 1823
American atlas; the 1771 Britannica plate has "Nigrita." So "Nigritia" is the English of
his period and I kept it rather than modernise him. "Caricia" is caress; "touch" because "caress of the air" is a greetings card. *Monte*:
hill, as always.

**2**

> Yo sé de las historias viejas
> Del hombre y de sus rencillas;
> Y prefiero las abejas
> Volando en las campanillas.

I know of the old histories
of man and his quarrels;
and I prefer the bees
flying in the bluebells.

*Note.* "Rencillas" are grudges, squabbles, feuds — smaller than wars; "quarrels" is the
right size. "Campanillas" are bellflowers, little bells; in Cuba the word covers several
climbing flowers and I do not know which he saw, so "bluebells" is the English reader's
flower and is marked as a choice, not a fact. The bee again, third poem running.

**3**

> Yo sé del canto del viento
> En las ramas vocingleras:
> Nadie me diga que miento,
> Que lo prefiero de veras.

I know of the song of the wind
in the loud-mouthed branches:
let no one tell me I lie,
I truly do prefer it.

*Note.* "Vocingleras" — loud, clamorous, chattering, of people who talk too much — applied
to branches. "Loud-mouthed branches" is the literal and I like it too much to soften it.
The third line is him anticipating the reader's smirk; kept it as a command.

**4**

> Yo sé de un gamo aterrado
> Que vuelve al redil, y expira, -
> Y de un corazón cansado
> Que muere oscuro y sin ira.

I know of a terrified deer
that comes back to the fold, and dies —
and of a tired heart
that dies obscure and without anger.

*Note.* "Gamo" is a fallow deer, specifically; "deer" because the species is not the
point. "Redil" is a sheepfold — a deer going back to a fold is already strange in Spanish
and should stay strange. "Oscuro" — dark, obscure, unknown; "obscure" carries the sense of
dying unnoticed, which "dark" does not. "Sin ira" is the last thing in the poem and I gave
it the last position.

---

Four of four. The poem is the same shape four times — *yo sé de X, y prefiero Y* — and the
fourth time the preference is gone and only the knowing is left. I did not notice that until
I had the English side by side. Next: IX, cold re-read of III first.
