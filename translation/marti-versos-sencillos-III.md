# Martí, *Versos sencillos* III

Source: es.wikisource.org, *Versos sencillos/III*, raw wikitext fetched 2026-08-22 (stamp in
the journal). Twelve quatrains. Same four rules as for I. All twelve done in one sitting; read once warm (nothing cut), then once ten minutes colder (one preposition changed, stanza 5).

Two words from poem I come straight back in the first stanza and I get to test my earlier
choices against them: *manso* (I took "gentle" in I.13) and *monte* (I took "hill" in I.2).

---

**1**

> Odio la máscara y vicio
> Del corredor de mi hotel:
> Me vuelvo al manso bullicio
> De mi monte de laurel.

I hate the mask and the vice
of the corridor of my hotel:
I go back to the gentle racket
of my hill of laurel.

*Note.* "Manso bullicio" — a tame uproar, a gentle din. "Gentle racket" keeps the
contradiction, which is the line. "Hill" again for *monte*, consistent with I.2; "mountain"
would have made the laurel grand and Martí is making it small and his. And: he hates the
*mask* in the hotel corridor. In I.1 I translated "sincero" as "without a mask," breaking my
own rule against adding an image, and marked the debt. Here is the word, his, two poems
later. I am calling the debt paid.

**2**

> Con los pobres de la tierra
> Quiero yo mi suerte echar:
> El arroyo de la sierra
> Me complace más que el mar.

With the poor of the earth
I want to cast my lot:
the stream in the sierra
pleases me more than the sea.

*Note.* "Echar" again, the verb from I.1 ("echar mis versos"), now "echar mi suerte" — cast
one's lot. Throw, cast, pour: the same hand. "Sierra" stays, because English took the word.

**3**

> Denle al vano el oro tierno
> Que arde y brilla en el crisol:
> A mí denme el bosque eterno
> Cuando rompe en él el Sol.

Give the vain man the soft gold
that burns and shines in the crucible:
give me the everlasting wood
when the sun breaks into it.

*Note.* "Oro tierno" — tender gold, soft gold, gold that is young — I took "soft," which is
what molten gold is and what *tierno* means of meat and of children both. "Bosque eterno":
"eternal forest" is a poster; "everlasting wood" is what a person says.

**4**

> Yo he visto el oro hecho tierra
> Barbullendo en la redoma:
> Prefiero estar en la sierra
> Cuando vuela una paloma.

I have seen gold turned to earth
bubbling in the flask:
I would rather be in the sierra
when a dove goes by.

*Note.* "Barbullendo" is a word I had to look at twice — bubbling, burbling, the noise of a
boil — onomatopoeic in Spanish and "bubbling" is the nearest English that is also a sound.
"Redoma" is an alchemist's flask. "Vuela" is flies; "goes by" because the dove is not the
point, the moment of its passing is.

**5**

> Busca el obispo de España
> Pilares para su altar;
> ¡En mi templo, en la montaña,
> El álamo es el pilar!

The bishop of Spain goes looking
for pillars for his altar;
in my temple, on the mountain,
the poplar is the pillar!

*Note.* "Montaña" here, not *monte* — the full word, and so "mountain" for the first time.
He uses the small word for his own hill and the big one for the temple. Kept the split.
*Cold read:* "in the mountain" was the Spanish *en* carried over; English puts a temple *on* a
mountain. Changed. Stanza 12 keeps "to the mountain" because *a la* is motion.

**6**

> Y la alfombra es puro helecho,
> Y los muros abedul,
> Y la luz viene del techo,
> Del techo de cielo azul.

And the carpet is all fern,
and the walls are birch,
and the light comes from the roof,
from the roof of blue sky.

*Note.* Four "and"s in Spanish (three *y* plus the repeated *del techo*); kept every one.
Nothing to translate here but the refusal to improve it.

**7**

> El obispo, por la noche,
> Sale, despacio, a cantar:
> Monta, callado, en su coche,
> Que es la piña de un pinar.

The bishop, at night,
goes out, slowly, to sing:
climbs, silent, into his coach,
which is the cone of a pine wood.

*Note.* The bishop is now a bird, or the poem's own priest, in a pinecone coach. "Callado"
— silent — is the *callar* of I.15 and I.18 again; the word keeps arriving. "Piña" is
pinecone (and pineapple; not here).

**8**

> Las jacas de su carroza
> Son dos pájaros azules:
> Y canta el aire y retoza,
> Y cantan los abedules.

The ponies of his carriage
are two blue birds:
and the air sings and frolics,
and the birches sing.

*Note.* "Jacas" are ponies, cobs, small horses. "Retoza" — romps, frolics, gambols; I
dislike all three English words and "frolics" least. Marked.

**9**

> Duermo en mi cama de roca
> Mi sueño dulce y profundo:
> Roza una abeja mi boca
> Y crece en mi cuerpo el mundo.

I sleep in my bed of rock
my sweet and deep sleep:
a bee brushes my mouth
and the world grows in my body.

*Note.* The bee from I.8 — there it stung a child and was barbarous; here it grazes the
sleeper's mouth and the world grows. Same animal, opposite stanza. "Roza" is brush, graze,
touch lightly; "brushes" for the wing.

**10**

> Brillan las grandes molduras
> Al fuego de la mañana
> Que tiñe las colgaduras
> De rosa, violeta y grana.

The great mouldings shine
in the fire of the morning
that dyes the hangings
rose, violet and scarlet.

*Note.* "Grana" is the red of cochineal — grain-dye, the old word. "Scarlet" is the
nearest English with a history; "crimson" would do too. The stanza is a sunrise described
as a church interior, which is the whole poem's trick done in four lines.

**11**

> El clarín, solo en el monte,
> Canta al primer arrebol:
> La gasa del horizonte
> Prende, de un aliento, el Sol.

The bugle, alone on the hill,
sings to the first red of dawn:
the gauze of the horizon
the sun lights with one breath.

*Note.* "Clarín" is a bugle or clarion. I had written "and also a songbird in Cuba — I am
told," and then checked: the birds called *clarín* (Myadestes solitaires) are Mexican and
Central American; the Cuban one, M. elisabeth, is called *ruiseñor* (es.wikipedia,
"Anexo: Aves de Cuba"; iNaturalist). So for a Cuban in 1891 the word is the instrument, and
"bugle" stands without the hedge. The hedge was a memory wearing a footnote. "Arrebol" is the red glow of clouds at sunrise or
sunset; one word in Spanish, five in English. "Prende" — lights, kindles, catches — the sun
setting fire to gauze with a breath. *Monte* again: "hill," consistent.

**12**

> ¡Díganle al obispo ciego,
> Al viejo obispo de España
> Que venga, que venga luego,
> A mi templo, a la montaña!

Tell the blind bishop,
the old bishop of Spain,
to come, to come soon,
to my temple, to the mountain!

*Note.* "Luego" is "later" in most Spanish and "soon, at once" in this older use; the
repetition "que venga, que venga" makes it urgent, so "soon." The last word is *montaña*
again, the big one, and so "mountain," which closes the split opened in stanza 5.
