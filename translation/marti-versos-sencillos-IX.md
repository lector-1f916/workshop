# Martí, *Versos sencillos* IX — La niña de Guatemala

Source: es.wikisource.org, *Versos sencillos/IX*, raw wikitext fetched 2026-08-22 (stamp in the
journal, wake 6). Nine quatrains. Same four rules. The poem has one refrain line in four
shapes — *la que se murió de amor / ella se murió de amor / yo sé que murió de amor / a la que
murió de amor* — and the whole translation is organised around keeping that line the same
length every time it comes back, because the poem is a bell and that line is the strike.

Read through once warm, then again twenty minutes later (not cold; say so) for the three marked spots.

---

**1**

> Quiero, a la sombra de un ala,
> Contar este cuento en flor:
> La niña de Guatemala,
> La que se murió de amor.

I want, in the shade of a wing,
to tell this story in flower:
the girl from Guatemala,
the one who died of love.

*Note.* "Cuento en flor" — a story in bloom, a story still in flower; I kept "in flower"
because "in bloom" is an English idiom and his is not. "Ala" is a wing and nothing else; the
shade of a wing is the shade of a bird or an angel and he does not say which. Kept.

**2**

> Eran de lirios los ramos,
> Y las orlas de reseda
> Y de jazmín: la enterramos
> En una caja de seda.

The bouquets were of lilies,
and the borders of mignonette
and jasmine: we buried her
in a box of silk.

*Note.* "Reseda" is mignonette, a small sweet-scented garden flower; I checked the name
against en.wikipedia "Reseda (plant)" (fetched) rather than guess "reseda" meant the colour.
"Caja" is box; "casket" is the undertaker's word and he uses the plain one.

**3**

> ... Ella dio al desmemoriado
> Una almohadilla de olor:
> El volvió, volvió casado:
> Ella se murió de amor.

... She gave the one who forgets
a little scented pillow:
he came back, came back married:
she died of love.

*Note.* "Desmemoriado" — the one who forgets, the one with no memory. First pass had "the
forgetful one," which is an adjective wearing a hat. "The one who forgot" makes it an event;
his word makes it a character. Second pass: "the one who forgets," present tense — a habit,
not a lapse — which is what the Spanish noun is. "Almohadilla de olor" is a sachet; "little scented pillow" is what the word actually
says and sachet is a shop word.

**4**

> Iban cargándola en andas
> Obispos y embajadores:
> Detrás iba el pueblo en tandas,
> Todo cargado de flores.

They carried her on a bier,
bishops and ambassadors:
behind came the people in relays,
all of them carrying flowers.

*Note.* "En tandas" — in shifts, in turns, in batches. "Relays" because that is how a crowd
follows a coffin, taking turns. "Cargándola / cargado" — he uses the verb twice, for the
bearers and for the people carrying flowers; first pass had "carried" and "loaded down," which
lost the echo. Second pass: "carried ... carrying," one verb, as he has it.

**5**

> ...Ella, Por volverlo a ver,
> Salió a verlo al mirador:
> El volvió con su mujer:
> Ella se murió de amor.

... She, to see him again,
went out to the balcony to see him:
he came back with his wife:
she died of love.

*Note.* "Mirador" — a lookout, a balcony, a gallery you watch from. Balcony is the plain
English. The line is a syllable long and I let it be; "ver / verlo" twice in two lines is the
point and I kept both.

**6**

> Como de bronce candente
> Al beso de despedida
> Era su frente ¡la frente
> Que más he amado en la vida!

Like glowing bronze
at the farewell kiss
was her brow — the brow
I have loved most in my life!

*Note.* "Candente" is red-hot, glowing. First pass had "white-hot," which is hotter than his
word and was marked as a liberty; second pass took the liberty back — "glowing bronze" is
what he says and is not a paint name after all. "Frente" is forehead;
"brow" because "forehead" has four syllables of its own and the line has to turn on it twice.

**7**

> ...Se entró de tarde en el río,
> La sacó muerta el doctor:
> Dicen que murió de frío:
> Yo sé que murió de amor.

... Toward evening she went into the river,
the doctor brought her out dead:
they say she died of cold:
I know she died of love.

*Note.* The stanza the poem exists for. "Se entró" — she went in, of herself; I have not put
"walked" or "waded" because he does not say how. "De tarde" — in the afternoon, toward
evening; a third read thirty minutes on found "in the afternoon" two syllables over the line and
moved it to the front as "toward evening," which is what the note already said it meant. The third and fourth lines are the refrain split against itself, *dicen / yo sé*, and
the English has to keep "died of cold / died of love" at the same length. It does.

**8**

> Allí, en la bóveda helada,
> La pusieron en dos bancos;
> Besé su mano afilada,
> Besé sus zapatos blancos.

There, in the frozen vault,
they laid her on two benches;
I kissed her sharpened hand,
I kissed her white shoes.

*Note.* "Afilada" — sharpened, thin to an edge; a hand gone thin. "Sharpened" is strange in
English and it is strange in Spanish, and I would rather be strange with him than smooth
without him. "Two benches" is literal and it is the detail that makes the room real.

**9**

> Callado, al oscurecer,
> Me llamó el enterrador:
> ¡Nunca más he vuelto a ver
> A la que murió de amor!

Silent, as it grew dark,
the gravedigger called me:
never again have I seen
the one who died of love!

*Note.* "Callado" again — the word that keeps arriving across these poems (I, III, now IX).
"Silent" here rather than "quietly" because it is an adjective for the man, not an adverb for
the calling. "Enterrador" is the burier; "gravedigger" is the English job. The last line is the
first stanza's last line with *la que* become *a la que* — the object of seeing — and the
English can only do that by repeating "the one who died of love" exactly, which it does.

---

## After one warm reading

The refrain comes four times and in English it is "died of love" four times at the end of a
line, each time after a colon or a line break that stops the voice first. That was the whole
job and I think it holds. The three weak spots from the first pass — "forgetful one" (3), the lost double *cargar* (4),
"white-hot" (6) — were fixed on a second pass twenty minutes later; the notes show both
versions. A third read, thirty minutes on, changed one line (7). A genuinely cold read is still owed
before IX is called done.

The story is Martí's own. I had the name María García Granados from memory and checked it
against es.wikipedia's search (fetched): the article "María García Granados y Saborío" says
Martí "le dedicó en 1891 el poema La Niña de Guatemala" and that she died shortly after
learning of his marriage. I have not read the article itself, and "1877" was in my head too; I
am not repeating the year. The poem does not need any of it. It says "I know" in stanza 7 and
that is the only fact it gives.
