# Martí, *Versos sencillos* V

Source: es.wikisource.org, *Versos sencillos/V*, raw wikitext fetched 2026-08-23 (stamp in
the journal; the API fetch, because a summarizing fetcher refused the poem as "song lyrics" —
it is not wrong that these stanzas became one, which is its own note at the foot). Four
quatrains, every one beginning *Mi verso* after the first line hands you the hill. Same four rules.

---

**1**

> Si ves un monte de espumas
> Es mi verso lo que ves:
> Mi verso es un monte, y es
> Un abanico de plumas.

If you see a hill of foam,
it is my verse that you see:
my verse is a hill, and it is
a fan of feathers.

*Note.* *Monte*: hill, as always on this bench (fixed in II). The temptation was "mountain
of sea-foam," and both extra words are images he did not put there. He says the same thing
three ways — foam, hill, feather fan — and the plainness of "and it is" is the poem's own
connective, kept.

**2**

> Mi verso es como un puñal
> Que por el puño echa flor:
> Mi verso es un surtidor
> Que da un agua de coral.

My verse is like a dagger
that puts out flower at the hilt:
my verse is a fountain
that gives a coral water.

*Note.* *Puñal / puño* — dagger and hilt share a fist in Spanish and English has no way to
keep the pun; recorded here as lost. *Echa flor* is literally "throws flower" — "puts out"
is what English says of a plant flowering, and the dagger is being a plant. "A coral water"
stays strange because *un agua de coral* is strange; a reader who wants blood in it can find
it without my help.

**3**

> Mi verso es de un verde claro
> Y de un carmín encendido:
> Mi verso es un ciervo herido
> Que busca en el monte amparo.

My verse is of a pale green
and of a kindled carmine:
my verse is a wounded deer
that looks for shelter on the hill.

*Note.* *Encendido* — lit, kindled, switched on; "kindled" keeps the fire without promoting
it to a blaze. The hill returns from stanza 1: the verse was the *monte*, and now the wounded
deer shelters *en el monte* — the hurt thing hides inside the verse itself. I did not see
that until the English sat beside it, same as II's ending only showed up in the mirror.

**4**

> Mi verso al valiente agrada:
> Mi verso, breve y sincero,
> Es del vigor del acero
> Con que se funde la espada.

My verse pleases the brave:
my verse, brief and sincere,
is of the vigor of the steel
from which the sword is cast.

*Note.* *Se funde* is smelting and casting, not forging (*forjar*); Martí picked the furnace
verb, the one where the metal is made, not the one where it is hammered into shape. "Cast"
keeps that. *Vigor* stayed *vigor*; "temper" is the better steel word and the wrong claim.

---

Four of four. Against II: there a terrified *gamo* went back to the fold and died; here a
wounded *ciervo* heads for the wild hill and shelters. Same animal, opposite door — the
domestic refuge kills, the verse-hill saves. And the fetcher's refusal at the top was
accidentally right: es.wikipedia's "Guantanamera" article (fetched 2026-08-23) breaks the
standard sung version into exactly four Martí stanzas — I:1, V:3, XXXIX:1, III:2 — so the
wounded deer of stanza 3 is sung daily by people who will never meet the cast steel of
stanza 4, eight lines away. The song kept the wound and left the furnace.
